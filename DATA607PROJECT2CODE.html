Step 2C: Normalize the variable structure. The stacked data mix two different kinds of observation: a monthly 12-month rate (one per year and month) and an annual average (one per year). Keeping them in the same column would break the rule that each row is one observation of one kind. They are therefore split into two tidy tables that can be joined on year.

annual_raw  <- long_all |> filter(period == "avg_annual") |> select(year, value_raw)
monthly_raw <- long_all |> filter(period != "avg_annual") |> rename(month = period)

Step 2D: Handle missing and inconsistent values. Three kinds of cell are not numeric. The decisions are documented in the table below and applied by classify_cell():

Raw cell Meaning Decision
Avail. Oct. 14 or blank Release has not happened yet rate_pct = NA, status = "pending_release"
– (*) BLS did not publish (2025 appropriations lapse) rate_pct = NA, status = "not_published"
A number written as text Observed value Convert to numeric, status = "observed"

No value is imputed or interpolated. Filling in October 2025 would invent a statistic that the BLS never published. The rows are kept (rather than dropped with drop_na()) so the gaps remain visible, and analyses filter on !is.na(rate_pct).

# Classify each raw text cell into one of four statuses
classify_cell <- function(x) {
  case_when(
    is.na(x) | str_trim(x) == ""                          ~ "pending_release",
    str_detect(x, "^\\s*-?[0-9]+(\\.[0-9]+)?\\s*$")       ~ "observed",
    str_detect(x, regex("avail", ignore_case = TRUE))     ~ "pending_release",
    str_detect(x, fixed("(*)"))                           ~ "not_published",
    TRUE                                                  ~ "unparsed"
  )
}

monthly_data <- monthly_raw |>
  mutate(
    status   = classify_cell(value_raw),
    # suppressWarnings(): non-numeric text becomes NA by design, and is
    # already labeled in `status`
    rate_pct = if_else(
      status == "observed",
      suppressWarnings(as.numeric(str_trim(value_raw))),
      NA_real_

3. Analytical methods

The analysis is descriptive. The questions come from my Discussion 5A post:

  1. Before and after the spike. Summary statistics (count, mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum) of the 12-month rate for each period, plus a time-series line chart of the full monthly series to show how quickly the rate rose and fell.

  2. December versus annual average. For each year with both values, compare the December 12-month rate with the BLS annual average and compute the gap in percentage points (pp). This shows how much year-to-year movement the annual average hides.

  3. Seasonal pattern. For each complete year (2000 to 2024), subtract that year’s mean rate from each month, then average these deviations by calendar month. A positive value means that month tends to run above its own year’s average.

  4. Other views. A heatmap of all months, a box plot of the rate within each period, and a bar chart of the annual average compared with 2%. These show the same tidy data from different angles and add no new calculations beyond medians and standard deviations.

Months without a numeric value are excluded from every calculation.

4. Conclusions

  • The 2021 to 2022 spike was large and fast. The 12-month rate rose from 1.4% in January 2021 to 9.1% in June 2022 and then dropped to 3.0% by June 2023. The descent (6.1 pp in 12 months) was quicker than the climb (7.7 pp in 17 months).
  • Inflation settled above its old norm. Post-spike months average 3.28% compared with 2.13% before 2021. In 2026 the rate rose again, from 2.4% in January and February to 4.2% in May, before easing to 3.4% in July and August.
  • The annual average hides the timing. December 2021 was 2.3 pp above that year’s average of 4.7%, and December 2022 was 1.5 pp below the average of 8.0%. The largest gap in the whole series is in 2008 (-3.7 pp). Anyone reading only the annual average would place the peak in 2022 and miss that inflation was already falling by December.
  • Seasonality is weak in this data. Monthly deviations stay within about 0.03 pp of the yearly mean, because 12-month changes remove most seasonal effects.
  • Limitations. The series is not seasonally adjusted, October 2025 is missing because of the 2025 lapse in appropriations (it was left as NA, not imputed), and September to December 2026 had not been released when the data were collected. The numbers are descriptive and do not explain causes.
